    How the lab computes pH

    No shortcut formulas inside. At every volume the lab solves the full charge balance [H⁺] + Σ cations = [OH⁻] + Σ anions. Every acid–base species takes its share from its αₖ fraction, and the equation is solved numerically for [H⁺]. That is why one engine handles HCl, H₃PO₄, glycine, NaOH + Na₂CO₃ mixtures and weak-acid/weak-base pairs.

    The JEE shortcuts are checked against it. The "Where am I" card picks the textbook formula for your region (½(pKₐ − log C), Henderson–Hasselbalch, ½(pKₐ₁ + pKₐ₂), salt hydrolysis, excess titrant) and shows how far it is from the exact value. Try very dilute solutions, the first few drops, or HCN to see where the shortcuts break.

    Temperature changes Kw (pKw is 14.94 at 0 °C and 13.02 at 60 °C), so neutral pH moves too. The pKₐ values of the acids are held at their 25 °C values.

    Activity. With Davies corrections on, the solver iterates the ionic strength I, builds conditional constants from the activity coefficients γ, and reports pH = −log a(H⁺), which is what a pH meter reads.

    Equivalence points come from stoichiometry. Each one is rated by its jump in pH across ±1 % of Veq. An indicator is listed as suitable when its pKIn falls inside the jump across ±0.5 %, and the endpoint error is where the curve actually crosses pKIn.

    Gran plot. Before the first equivalence point, (V₀+V)·10−pH for a strong acid, or V·10−pH = Kₐ(Veq − V) for a weak one, is linear in V and reaches zero at Veq. Fit your own drops and extrapolate. Analysts use this to find Veq without ever reaching it.
