1 · The NEET core (learn these cold)

  • Resting membrane potential ≈ −70 mV (inside negative). The membrane is polarised. HH's squid axon rests at ≈ −65 mV; the simulator uses that.
  • At rest the axolemma is more permeable to K⁺ and nearly impermeable to Na⁺ and to the negatively charged proteins inside.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in for every ATP. That keeps the concentration gradients (Na⁺ high outside, K⁺ high inside) and makes the pump slightly electrogenic.
  • A stimulus opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. Na⁺ rushes in, the inner surface turns positive (≈ +30 to +40 mV) and the membrane is depolarised.
  • Repolarisation happens because Na⁺ channels inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, so K⁺ flows out.
  • K⁺ channels close slowly, which causes a brief hyperpolarisation (undershoot).
  • All-or-none law: below threshold nothing propagates. Above threshold the spike has a fixed size, and a stronger stimulus gives a higher frequency of spikes, not taller ones.
  • Absolute refractory period: no stimulus, however strong, can fire a second spike (Na⁺ channels are inactivated). Relative refractory period: a stronger-than-normal stimulus can.
  • In myelinated fibres the impulse jumps between nodes of Ranvier (saltatory conduction). This is faster and uses less energy.

3 · The Hodgkin–Huxley model (1952, Nobel 1963)

Hodgkin and Huxley used a voltage clamp on the squid giant axon to measure Na⁺ and K⁺ currents separately. They then wrote down four coupled differential equations that reproduce the action potential. The membrane is treated as an electrical circuit: a capacitor (the lipid bilayer) in parallel with three conductances (ion channels), each with its own battery (Nernst potential).

Cm dV/dt = Istim − ḡNa m³h (V − ENa) − ḡK n⁴ (V − EK) − gL(V − EL)
dx/dt = φ [ αx(V)(1 − x) − βx(V) x ]   for x ∈ {m, h, n},    φ = 3(T − 6.3)/10
equivalently   dx/dt = (x∞(V) − x) / τx(V),   x∞ = α/(α+β),   τx = 1/(φ(α+β))
  • m³h: a Na⁺ channel conducts only if three independent activation particles (m) and one inactivation particle (h) are all in the permissive position. Each gate is a probability between 0 and 1.
  • n⁴: a K⁺ channel conducts only when four n particles are permissive. The fourth power makes K⁺ activation start with a delay (sigmoidal onset). That is why it is called the "delayed rectifier".
  • Parameters: ḡNa = 120, ḡK = 36, gL = 0.3 mS/cm²; Cm = 1 µF/cm²; EL = −54.4 mV.
  • The key is the separation of timescales. m is fast; h and n are about 10× slower. So Na⁺ turns on first (upstroke), then h shuts Na⁺ off while n turns K⁺ on (downstroke).

Left: steady-state gate values x∞(V). Right: time constants τx(V) at 6.3 °C. Note how m∞ and h∞ overlap only a little near −60 to −40 mV. This "window current" is part of what sets the threshold.

4 · Nernst & Goldman (GHK)

Eion = (RT / zF) · ln([ion]out / [ion]in)  ≈  61.5 log10([out]/[in]) mV at 37 °C (z = +1)
Vrest = (RT/F) · ln[ (PK[K⁺]o + PNa[Na⁺]o + PCl[Cl⁻]i) / (PK[K⁺]i + PNa[Na⁺]i + PCl[Cl⁻]o) ]

Worked example (mammalian neuron, 37 °C): [K⁺]o = 5, [K⁺]i = 140 mM gives EK = 61.5·log(5/140) ≈ −89 mV. [Na⁺]o = 145, [Na⁺]i = 12 gives ENa ≈ +67 mV. With PK : PNa ≈ 1 : 0.04, GHK gives about −70 mV. Rest sits close to EK because K⁺ permeability dominates.

At the peak of the spike the permeability ratio flips to roughly PNa : PK ≈ 20 : 1, so V moves toward ENa. The action potential is a temporary change in relative permeability. Very few ions actually cross: about 10⁻⁵ of the internal K⁺ per spike. Concentrations barely change, so a pump-poisoned axon can still fire thousands of spikes.

5 · Beyond the textbook (try each in the simulator)

Threshold is not a fixed voltage

In HH there is no hard-wired "−55 mV" switch. Threshold is the point where inward Na⁺ current starts to outgrow outward K⁺ + leak current, so it depends on stimulus duration, h, n and the stimulus history. The strength–duration curve (Analysis panel) shows this: short pulses need much more current. Rheobase is the minimum current for an infinitely long pulse. Chronaxie is the duration needed at 2× rheobase.

Why refractoriness exists

ARP: h is near 0, so almost every Na⁺ channel is inactivated and no stimulus can produce regenerative inward current. RRP: h is only partly recovered and n is still high (extra K⁺ conductance), so you need a bigger push. The simulator shades these bands using h and n recovery, calibrated against paired-pulse tests (≈ 2.5 ms ARP, ≈ 10 ms RRP at 6.3 °C). Refractoriness is also why impulses travel one way along an axon.

Anode-break excitation

A long hyperpolarising pulse removes Na⁺ inactivation (h rises above rest) and closes K⁺ channels (n falls). When the pulse ends, the membrane rebounds into a now hyper-excitable state and fires. A spike from a negative stimulus surprises most students.

Depolarisation block & hyperkalaemia

Strong sustained depolarisation keeps h near 0, so Na⁺ channels stay inactivated and firing stops. Raising [K⁺]o makes EK less negative, which depolarises rest and inactivates Na⁺ channels. This is why IV KCl stops the heart and why hyperkalaemia causes muscle weakness and arrhythmias.

Temperature

Gating speeds up by Q10 = 3. Warmer means narrower, faster spikes. Push too far and K⁺ activation catches up with Na⁺ activation before the upstroke completes: heat block (≈ 30 °C for squid parameters).

Type II excitability

The HH neuron cannot fire arbitrarily slowly. At about 6.3 µA/cm² it jumps from silence straight to ≈ 50 Hz (a subcritical Hopf bifurcation). The f–I curve shows this jump. Many cortical neurons are "type I" instead, and can fire at very low rates.

Toxins & drugs

  • Tetrodotoxin (TTX), from puffer fish, plugs voltage-gated Na⁺ channels: no action potential. Saxitoxin works the same way. Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine also block Na⁺ channels.
  • Tetraethylammonium (TEA) blocks voltage-gated K⁺ channels, so repolarisation becomes very slow and the spike turns into a plateau.

Conduction velocity

Velocity grows with axon diameter (∝ √d unmyelinated, ∝ d myelinated). Myelin lowers capacitance and raises membrane resistance, which increases the length constant λ = √(rm/ri). Current then spreads passively to the next node, where Na⁺ channels are concentrated. Squid axons (≈ 0.5 mm) reach ≈ 25 m/s without myelin. A 10 µm myelinated mammalian fibre reaches ≈ 60 m/s.

6 · NEET traps

"Repolarisation is due to the Na⁺/K⁺ pump." False. It is due to Na⁺ inactivation + voltage-gated K⁺ efflux. The pump restores gradients over the long term.
"A stronger stimulus gives a bigger action potential." False (all-or-none). Stronger means more frequent spikes, or more fibres recruited in a nerve.
"During depolarisation the outside becomes positive." False. The inside becomes positive, and the outer surface becomes negative relative to it.
"Saltatory conduction happens in non-myelinated fibres." False. It happens only in myelinated fibres, from node to node.
"Na⁺ enters by active transport during the AP." False. It moves passively down its electrochemical gradient through channels.
